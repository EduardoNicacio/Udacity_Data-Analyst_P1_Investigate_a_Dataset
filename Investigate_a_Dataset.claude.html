1. Introduction

Dataset Description

The No-Show Appointments dataset records 110,527 medical appointments from public health clinics in Vitória, Espírito Santo (Brazil), scheduled between November 2015 and June 2016. Each row represents a single appointment and captures patient demographics, health characteristics, scheduling metadata, and whether the patient attended.

⚠️ Critical encoding note: The No-show column is counterintuitively labelled. 'Yes' means the patient did NOT attend; 'No' means the patient did attend. This is corrected during wrangling by creating a clean binary column (no_show = 1 for missed appointments).

Research Questions

  1. RQ1 — Patient Demographics & Health: Which patient characteristics (age group, gender, scholarship enrolment, chronic conditions) are associated with higher appointment no-show rates?
  2. RQ2 — Waiting Time: How does the number of days between scheduling and the appointment date affect the probability of a no-show?
  3. RQ3 — SMS Reminders: Does receiving an SMS reminder reduce the no-show rate, and does its effectiveness vary with waiting time?

All 14 columns are fully populated (no NaN values). One row contains Age = −1 — a data-entry error — which is removed. A small number of rows have wait_days < 0 (appointment timestamp precedes scheduling timestamp), which are also removed as physically impossible.

Cleaning Steps Applied

  1. Renamed columns to snake_case for ergonomics (Hipertension → hypertension, etc.).
  2. Parsed date columns (ScheduledDay, AppointmentDay) from ISO-8601 strings to datetime objects with UTC timezone.
  3. Re-encoded target variable: Created no_show (1 = missed, 0 = attended) from the counterintuitive original string encoding.
  4. Engineered wait_days: appointment_day − scheduled_day in calendar days.
  5. Removed errant rows: 1 row with age = −1; rows with wait_days < 0.
  6. Dropped identifiers: patient_id and appointment_id carry no analytical signal.
  7. Binned features: age_group (0–17 / 18–39 / 40–59 / 60+) and wait_category (Same Day / 1–7 Days / 8–30 Days / 31+ Days).

3. Exploratory Data Analysis

For each research question we perform both 1-D analysis (distribution of individual variables) and 2-D analysis (relationship with the no-show rate). Two reusable helper functions — plot_noshow_rate() and describe_group() — are defined once and called across all three research questions to ensure consistent formatting and pair every visualisation with numeric evidence.

RQ1 Key Findings:
  • Gender has virtually no effect (~20% for both F and M). Not a useful predictor.
  • Young adults (18–39) no-show at the highest rate (~22%); children and elderly patients attend more reliably.
  • Scholarship enrolment is associated with a higher no-show rate (~24% vs ~20%). Logistical barriers likely play a role.
  • Chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes) are associated with lower no-show rates (~17%), consistent with higher perceived healthcare urgency.
These are associations only — no causal claims are made.

RQ2 — How Does Waiting Time Affect the No-Show Rate?

wait_days was engineered as appointment_day − scheduled_day. We first examine its distribution (1-D), then aggregate into four scheduling-lag bands (2-D).

1-D: Distribution of Waiting Days (histogram, 0–60 days shown)

Distribution is strongly right-skewed. Median: 4 days · Mean: 10.2 days · ~21% of appointments are same-day (0 days).

2-D: No-Show Rate by Waiting Time Category (bar chart)
RQ2 Key Findings:
  • Waiting time is the strongest factor associated with no-show rates across all variables examined.
  • Same-day appointments: no-show rate < 5%.
  • 1–7 day waits: ~17%; 8–30 day waits: ~25%; 31+ day waits: > 31%.
  • The majority of appointments fall within 14 days (right-skewed distribution), but the high-risk long-wait tail accounts for a disproportionate share of no-shows.

RQ3 — Does Receiving an SMS Reminder Reduce the No-Show Rate?

We examine no-show by sms_received (2-D), then add wait_category as a second dimension to investigate whether the apparent SMS effect is confounded by scheduling lag.

2-D: No-Show Rate by SMS Reminder Status (bar chart)
2-D Interaction: No-Show Rate by Waiting Time × SMS Status (grouped bar)
RQ3 Key Findings — The SMS Paradox:
  • Raw figures show SMS recipients have a higher no-show rate (27.6%) than non-recipients (16.7%) — an apparent paradox.
  • The interaction plot explains this: SMS reminders were predominantly sent for long-wait appointments, which independently carry the highest no-show risk. SMS coverage rises from near-zero for same-day slots to over 50% for 31+ day waits.
  • When wait category is held constant, the SMS group shows a slightly higher no-show rate within each tier — suggesting the reminder alone does not fully overcome the risk of long scheduling gaps.
  • SMS reminders appear necessary but not sufficient: additional interventions (shorter scheduling windows, proactive calls, automated rescheduling) are likely needed.
Correlation does not imply causation. Whether SMS assignment was random is unknown; patients who provided phone numbers may differ systematically from those who did not.

Limitations

  1. Observational data — no causal inference. All findings are associations. We cannot conclude that long wait times cause no-shows without a controlled experiment.
  2. Missing predictors. Appointment type (routine vs. specialist vs. urgent), transport access, employment status, and time-of-day are absent and likely explain substantial residual variance.
  3. Geographic and temporal scope. All appointments are from a single Brazilian city (Vitória, Espírito Santo) in 2015–2016. Findings may not generalise to other healthcare systems or time periods.
  4. SMS assignment mechanism unknown. Whether reminders were sent systematically or selectively is unclear; if assignment was non-random, the SMS comparison is biased in an unknown direction.

Directions for Future Research

  • Build a predictive classifier (logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees) using all identified variables to flag high-risk appointments for proactive outreach before they occur.
  • Test shortened scheduling windows as an intervention through a prospective field experiment with random assignment.
  • Link appointment records to patient health outcomes to determine whether no-shows lead to measurable deterioration in chronic-condition management.
