Introduction¶

Dataset Description¶

This project analyses the No-Show Appointments dataset (source: Kaggle / Brazilian Ministry of Health), which records 110,527 medical appointments scheduled in Vitória, Brazil, between 2015 and 2016. Each row describes a single appointment and whether the patient attended.

Column Type Description
PatientId int Unique patient identifier
AppointmentID int Unique appointment identifier
Gender str Patient gender: M or F
ScheduledDay str ISO-8601 timestamp when appointment was booked
AppointmentDay str ISO-8601 timestamp of the appointment itself
Age int Patient age in years
Neighbourhood str Hospital neighbourhood
Scholarship int 1 if enrolled in Bolsa Família welfare programme
Hipertension int 1 if patient has hypertension
Diabetes int 1 if patient has diabetes
Alcoholism int 1 if patient has alcoholism
Handcap int Handicap severity 0–4
SMS_received int 1 if patient received an SMS reminder
No-show str 'Yes' = did NOT attend; 'No' = DID attend ⚠️ counterintuitive

⚠️ Encoding note: The No-show column is counterintuitively labelled. 'Yes' means the patient missed the appointment; 'No' means they attended. We correct this during wrangling.

Research Questions¶

  1. RQ1 — Patient Demographics & Health: Which patient characteristics (age group, gender, social-welfare enrolment, and chronic-condition status) are associated with higher no-show rates?
  2. RQ2 — Waiting Time: How does the number of days between scheduling and the appointment date affect the probability of a no-show?
  3. RQ3 — SMS Reminders: Does receiving an SMS reminder reduce the no-show rate, and does its effectiveness vary with waiting time?

Data Cleaning¶

Based on the inspection above, the following cleaning steps are applied:

  1. Rename columns to snake_case for ergonomics.
  2. Convert date columns (ScheduledDay, AppointmentDay) from raw strings to datetime objects.
  3. Re-encode the target: Create a binary no_show column (1 = missed, 0 = attended) from the counterintuitive original.
  4. Engineer wait_days: Days elapsed between scheduling and the appointment.
  5. Remove errant rows: Drop the single row with age == -1 and any rows where wait_days < 0 (appointment logged before it was scheduled — likely data-entry errors).
  6. Drop identifiers: PatientId and AppointmentID carry no analytical signal.
  7. Engineer categorical bins: age_group (four life-stage bands) and wait_category (four scheduling-lag bands) for grouped analysis.

def plot_noshow_rate(data, column, title, xlabel,
                    figsize=(10, 5), colors=None):
    """
    Compute and plot the no-show rate (%) for each category in `column`.
    Adds a horizontal reference line for the overall rate.
    Returns a Series of rates (index = category label, values = %).
    Called for every categorical variable explored across RQ1, RQ2, RQ3.
    """
    rates    = data.groupby(column, observed=True)['no_show'].mean() * 100
    n_cats   = len(rates)
    palette  = colors if colors else plt.cm.tab10.colors[:n_cats]

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=figsize)
    bars = ax.bar(rates.index.astype(str), rates.values,
                  color=palette, edgecolor='black', width=0.6)

    # Value labels above each bar
    for bar in bars:
        ax.text(bar.get_x() + bar.get_width() / 2.,
                bar.get_height() + 0.4,
                f'{bar.get_height():.1f}%',
                ha='center', va='bottom', fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')

    # Overall no-show rate reference line
    overall = data['no_show'].mean() * 100
    ax.axhline(overall, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=1.4,
               label=f'Overall rate ({overall:.1f}%)')

    ax.set_title(title)
    ax.set_xlabel(xlabel)
    ax.set_ylabel('No-Show Rate (%)')
    ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(mtick.PercentFormatter())
    ax.legend(fontsize=10)
    plt.xticks(rotation=45, ha='right')
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()
    return rates


def describe_group(data, column):
    """
    Print a concise statistical summary (total, no-shows, rate %) for
    each category of `column`.  Called alongside plot_noshow_rate for
    every variable explored to pair every chart with numeric evidence.
    """
    summary = data.groupby(column, observed=True).agg(
        total        = ('no_show', 'count'),
        no_shows     = ('no_show', 'sum'),
        no_show_rate = ('no_show', lambda x: round(x.mean() * 100, 2))
    )
    summary['no_show_rate'] = summary['no_show_rate'].map('{:.2f}%'.format)
    print(summary.to_string())
    return summary

Observations — RQ1:

  • Gender has virtually no effect on the no-show rate (Female ≈ Male ≈ 20%). Gender is not a useful predictor in isolation.
  • Age shows a non-linear pattern: young adults aged 18–39 have the highest no-show rate (~22%), while children (0–17) and elderly patients (60+) attend more reliably. The 18–39 group may have more flexible working schedules or lower perceived urgency.
  • Scholarship enrolment (Bolsa Família) is associated with a meaningfully higher no-show rate (~24% vs. ~20%). Patients in lower socioeconomic circumstances may face greater logistical barriers such as transport, childcare, or inflexible employment.
  • Chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes) are associated with lower no-show rates (~17%), consistent with the hypothesis that patients managing ongoing illness treat appointments with greater urgency. Alcoholism shows a slightly higher no-show rate.

⚠️ These are associations only — we make no causal claims.

Observations — RQ2:

  • Waiting time is the single strongest factor associated with no-show rates across all variables examined.
  • Same-day appointments have a no-show rate below 5% — patients who schedule and attend on the same day almost always appear.
  • No-show rates rise sharply with scheduling lag: approximately 17% for 1–7 day waits, 25% for 8–30 days, and over 30% for waits longer than a month.
  • The distribution of wait_days is right-skewed: the majority of appointments are within the first two weeks, but a long tail extends beyond 30 days — precisely the highest-risk group.

⚠️ We cannot conclude that long wait times cause no-shows — patients who self-select into distant appointment slots may differ systematically from those who book same-day.

Observations — RQ3:

  • Counterintuitive raw result: At first glance, patients who received an SMS have a higher no-show rate (~28%) than those who did not (~17%). This appears paradoxical.
  • Confounding explanation: The interaction plot reveals why. SMS reminders were disproportionately sent to patients with longer scheduling lags — the same high-lag appointments that independently carry the highest no-show rates. The SMS coverage rate rises from near-zero for same-day appointments to over 50% for 31+ day waits.
  • Within-tier comparison: When we hold wait category constant, the SMS-received group shows a slightly lower or comparable no-show rate in each tier — but the effect is modest (typically 2–4 percentage points). The reminder alone cannot fully overcome the risk created by a long scheduling gap.
  • The data suggest that SMS reminders reach the right patients (long-wait appointments) but that additional interventions — such as shorter scheduling windows, reminder calls, or automated rescheduling prompts — would be needed to substantially reduce no-shows.

⚠️ Correlation ≠ Causation: We do not know whether SMS assignment was random. Patients who provided a phone number may differ systematically from those who did not, which would bias this comparison.

Conclusions¶

Summary of Findings¶

This analysis examined over 110,000 Brazilian medical appointments to understand which factors are associated with patients missing their scheduled appointments (the "no-show" rate sits at approximately 20% overall).

Research Question Key Finding
RQ1 — Demographics & Health Young adults (18–39) have the highest no-show rate (~22%). Scholarship enrolment is associated with higher no-shows (~24%). Patients with hypertension or diabetes no-show less (~17%), suggesting health urgency drives attendance. Gender has negligible effect (~20% for both).
RQ2 — Waiting Time Waiting time is the strongest factor. Same-day no-show rate < 5%; 31+ day no-show rate > 30%. Reducing scheduling lag is the most actionable lever identified in this dataset.
RQ3 — SMS Reminders The raw no-show rate is higher among SMS recipients due to confounding with long wait times. Within each wait tier, SMS is associated with a modest 2–4 percentage point reduction. SMS alone is not sufficient to counteract the risk of long scheduling gaps.

Limitations¶

  1. Observational data — no causal inference: All findings are associations. We cannot conclude that long wait times cause no-shows, or that SMS reminders prevent them, without a randomised controlled study.
  2. Missing predictors: Appointment type (routine vs. specialist vs. urgent care), transport access, employment status, and time-of-day are absent. These likely explain substantial residual variance in no-show behaviour.
  3. Geographic & temporal scope: All appointments are from a single Brazilian city (Vitória, Espírito Santo) in 2015–2016. Findings may not generalise to other healthcare systems, cities, or time periods.
  4. SMS assignment mechanism unknown: Whether reminders were sent systematically or selectively is unclear. If assignment was not random, the SMS comparison is biased in an unknown direction.

Directions for Future Research¶

  • Build a predictive classifier (logistic regression or gradient boosted trees) using all identified variables to flag high-risk appointments before they occur.
  • Test shortened scheduling windows as an intervention through a prospective field experiment.
  • Link appointment records to patient health outcomes to determine whether no-shows lead to measurable deterioration in chronic-condition management.
