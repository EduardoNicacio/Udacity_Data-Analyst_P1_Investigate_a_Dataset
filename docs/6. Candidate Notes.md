## 1. Elevator Pitch

**The Problem:** The No-Show Appointments dataset contains 110,527 medical appointments from public health clinics in Vitória, Espírito Santo, Brazil, during 2015–2016. It includes patient demographics, health indicators, scheduling dates, SMS reminder status, and an outcome column whose encoding is easy to misread: `No` means the patient attended, while `Yes` means the patient did not attend. The analysis needed to move beyond raw counts and investigate three questions: which patient characteristics are associated with no-shows, how scheduling lead time relates to attendance, and how SMS reminders behave across different waiting-time groups.

**The Solution:** An executed Jupyter notebook implements a reproducible pandas/NumPy/Matplotlib exploratory analysis. It checks source data quality, standardizes column names, parses dates, derives a binary `no_show` target and `wait_days`, removes rows with negative ages or negative waiting times, drops identifiers, creates age and wait-time categories, and analyzes each research question with both distributions and group-level no-show rates. The notebook also includes reusable plotting and group-summary helpers, making the comparisons consistent across sections.

**The Result:** The source dataset has **110,527 rows × 14 columns**, no missing values, and no duplicate rows. The executed cleaning code removes **38,568 rows** with `age < 0` or `wait_days < 0`, leaving **71,959 rows × 13 columns**. In this retained dataset, **20,522 appointments are no-shows**, an overall rate of **28.52%**. The clearest observed patterns are higher no-show rates among patients aged 0–39, an increase from **21.35% for same-day appointments to 33.02% for waits of 31+ days**, and a lower overall no-show rate among SMS recipients (**27.57% vs. 29.44%**). Within longer waiting-time categories, the SMS group also has lower observed rates. These are associations, not proof of causation. The most important qualification is that the cleaning step removes a large share of the original data because of negative `wait_days`; the meaning and validity of that rule should be investigated before treating the cleaned dataset as representative.

### Key Design Decisions

| Decision | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Create an explicit `no_show` target** | The source label is counterintuitive: `No` means attended and `Yes` means missed. Re-encoding it as `no_show = 1` for a missed appointment makes group means interpretable as no-show rates. |
| **Standardize column names** | Snake-case names such as `scheduled_day`, `appointment_day`, and `sms_received` make subsequent transformations and plotting easier to read. |
| **Derive `wait_days` from dates** | Waiting time provides a directly interpretable scheduling interval and supports both numeric summaries and grouped comparisons. The precise date arithmetic and timezone handling should be validated because they affect which rows are considered negative. |
| **Remove negative ages and negative waits** | Negative age is clearly invalid. Negative waiting time is also physically suspicious, but the rule removes 38,568 rows—far more than the one negative-age row—so this decision needs deeper investigation and sensitivity analysis. |
| **Use interpretable age and wait-time bands** | Age bands (0–17, 18–39, 40–59, 60+) and wait bands (Same Day, 1–7, 8–30, 31+ days) make group comparisons easier to communicate. Results depend partly on these chosen boundaries. |
| **Pair distributions with no-show rates** | One-dimensional distributions describe who and what is represented; two-dimensional group rates connect those characteristics to the outcome. |
| **Analyze SMS jointly with wait category** | A raw SMS comparison alone can hide differences in which appointments received reminders. Stratifying by wait category reveals a more nuanced association, though it does not remove all confounding. |

## 3. Analytical Principles in Practice

**Check the data before interpreting it.** The notebook begins with dimensions, types, descriptive statistics, missing-value counts, duplicate checks, target counts, and an age-range spot check. These checks establish the source-data baseline before cleaning.

**Make target semantics explicit.** The original `No-show` label is not treated as if `Yes` meant attendance. Creating `no_show` with 1 for missed appointments ensures the mean of the binary target can be read as a rate.

**Separate transformation from interpretation.** The notebook derives dates, waiting days, and categories before using them in group-level summaries. This keeps the analytical questions tied to named, reusable features rather than repeated ad hoc expressions.

**Use both counts and rates.** A percentage without its denominator can be misleading. For example, the age-group comparisons include both appointment totals and no-show counts, while the waiting-time analysis reports counts and rates for each category.

**Look for interactions instead of relying on one-variable comparisons.** Overall SMS recipients have a lower no-show rate than non-recipients, and that pattern is also present within the longer wait categories. However, reminder coverage differs materially by wait category, so the overall difference should not be interpreted as a causal estimate of SMS effectiveness.

**Treat cleaning as an analytical decision, not a mechanical ritual.** Removing rows with negative waiting times may be reasonable if those values are confirmed to be invalid. But because 38,568 of 110,527 rows are removed, this decision is consequential and must be investigated rather than described as a trivial cleanup.

## 4. AI Collaboration Playbook

The project’s immediate deliverable was an HTML report whose claims needed to match the notebook’s executed cells. The notebook was treated as the source of truth for numeric findings rather than accepting the report’s existing prose and SVG chart labels at face value.

The workflow was:

1. Inspect the executed notebook outputs and capture the actual row counts, distributions, group rates, and SMS-by-wait results.
2. Compare those results with the HTML report, identifying stale figures and claims that contradicted the executed analysis.
3. Update the report’s text and numeric summaries using the notebook outputs.
4. Preserve the existing report structure and visual design while making clear that the original SVG chart geometry had not been comprehensively regenerated from the notebook plots.
5. Treat that remaining chart-validation gap as a publication caveat rather than claiming that all visualizations had been fully reconciled.

The key collaboration principle is **evidence-first editing**: use executed outputs to ground the narrative, avoid inventing results, and distinguish corrected text from visuals that still require direct validation. The notebook and the report should not be considered equally authoritative when their figures disagree; the executed notebook outputs are the reference for the figures used here.

## 5. Evaluation & Verification Philosophy

**Executed outputs are the evidence.** The key values below were taken from the notebook’s executed cells: source dimensions, data-quality checks, post-cleaning dimensions, no-show totals, demographic rates, waiting-time summaries, and SMS interaction results.

**Validate denominators as well as percentages.** The cleaned data has 71,959 rows and 20,522 no-shows. The reported 28.52% overall rate is consistent with those totals. Group comparisons similarly retain their totals and no-show counts.

**Check that narrative conclusions follow the table values.** For instance, gender rates differ by only 0.22 percentage points (28.45% for F and 28.67% for M), while waiting-time rates rise from 21.35% for same-day appointments to 33.02% for 31+ day waits. These support different levels of emphasis in the narrative.

**Do not infer causality from EDA.** The notebook is descriptive; it does not establish that age, waiting time, or SMS reminders cause attendance outcomes. SMS assignment may not be random, and patient, clinic, and scheduling factors may confound the comparisons.

**Keep data-retention caveats visible.** The 38,568-row removal is central to interpreting every result. Any claim about representativeness or generalizability should remain qualified until the negative-wait records and date logic are understood.

**Treat report rendering as a separate quality gate.** Correcting prose does not automatically correct SVG bar heights, axis labels, annotations, or embedded chart comments. The HTML charts should be checked against the executed notebook figures before the report is published or submitted.

## 6. Challenges & Resolutions

### Challenge 1 — The counterintuitive `No-show` label

**What happened:** The source column uses `No` for appointments attended and `Yes` for appointments missed. Reading the labels literally without checking their meaning would invert the target and make every finding wrong.

**Resolution:** The notebook documents the encoding and creates `no_show`, where 1 means a missed appointment and 0 means attended. All reported no-show rates use that derived target.

### Challenge 2 — A large number of rows removed during cleaning

**What happened:** The source data contains one row with a negative age, but the cleaning code removes all rows where `age < 0` or `wait_days < 0`, resulting in **38,568 removed rows** and a retained dataset of 71,959 rows. The negative-wait filter—not the negative-age row—is responsible for most of the reduction.

**Resolution:** The report now states the actual cleaning rule and row count rather than implying that only one errant age row was removed. This is a reporting correction, not proof that the filtering rule is analytically appropriate. The next step should investigate the raw timestamps, timezone conversion, calendar-day calculation, and distribution of negative waits; then compare findings under alternative defensible cleaning rules.

### Challenge 3 — Interpreting waiting-time associations

**What happened:** No-show rates rise across the chosen waiting-time groups: 21.35% for same-day appointments, 25.01% for 1–7 days, 32.00% for 8–30 days, and 33.02% for 31+ days. It is tempting to conclude that longer waits directly cause missed appointments.

**Resolution:** Present the result as a strong descriptive association and an actionable hypothesis for follow-up—not as a causal effect. Additional analysis should account for other possible drivers such as patient history, appointment type, clinic, day of week, and scheduling practices.

### Challenge 4 — The apparent SMS paradox and confounding

**What happened:** The overall no-show rate is 29.44% among appointments without SMS and 27.57% among appointments with SMS. The interaction table shows lower observed SMS-group rates for 1–7 days (24.25% vs. 25.62%), 8–30 days (29.19% vs. 36.08%), and 31+ days (30.33% vs. 37.50%). There are no SMS recipients in the same-day group, so that comparison is undefined.

**Resolution:** Report the actual rates and SMS coverage by wait category, and explicitly state that the comparison is observational. The pattern is compatible with a beneficial association, but it does not establish SMS effectiveness because reminder assignment and other differences may be confounded.

### Challenge 5 — Updating an HTML report without assuming its charts are correct

**What happened:** The original HTML report contained figures and narrative claims that differed from the notebook’s executed outputs. Updating visible text alone does not guarantee that embedded SVG bars and labels represent the new values.

**Resolution:** The report text and summary figures were updated using the notebook as the numeric reference. The remaining limitation was explicitly identified: the SVG chart geometry had not been comprehensively regenerated, so a final chart-by-chart verification remains necessary.

## 7. Review & Refinement

The principal refinement was reconciling the HTML report with the executed notebook rather than preserving figures from the original narrative. The corrected report reflects the retained dataset size (71,959 rows), the overall no-show rate (28.52%), the actual demographic and waiting-time comparisons, and the SMS-by-wait interaction.

The most important analytical refinement was making the cleaning impact explicit. The report must not describe the dataset as if only the negative-age record had been removed. The notebook’s code removes 38,568 rows under the combined negative-age/negative-wait rule. That fact affects the interpretation of every downstream percentage and deserves more attention than a cosmetic report correction.

The work also exposed a difference between **numeric reconciliation** and **visual reconciliation**. Text and displayed figures can be corrected while the SVG chart geometry remains stale. The updated report is therefore a corrected working artifact, but the visuals should be regenerated from the executed notebook or checked chart by chart before being treated as publication-ready.

## 10. Retrospective

### What I'd Do Differently

1. **Investigate negative waiting times before dropping them.** A reduction of 38,568 rows is too large to treat as routine cleaning. I would inspect raw `ScheduledDay` and `AppointmentDay` examples, confirm timezone assumptions and date arithmetic, quantify negative values by magnitude and source characteristics, and document a defensible retention rule. I would then run a sensitivity analysis to show how conclusions change under alternative treatments.

2. **Add an explicit cleaning audit table.** Each cleaning step should report rows before, rows removed, rows retained, and the reason for removal. This would make the impact of the negative-wait rule immediately visible and prevent a report from accidentally implying that only one age outlier was removed.

3. **Regenerate report charts from the same analysis outputs.** Rather than maintaining hand-authored SVG chart geometry separately from the notebook, I would export figures or generate charts from a shared results object. This reduces the risk that corrected text sits beside stale bars, labels, or annotations.

4. **Quantify uncertainty and adjust for confounders.** Group rates are useful for exploration but do not establish independent effects. A follow-up analysis could report confidence intervals and fit a carefully validated model with relevant patient, clinic, and scheduling covariates. Any SMS-effect estimate would need particular attention to selection bias and non-random reminder assignment.

5. **Check category sensitivity.** The age and wait-time bins are interpretable, but the boundaries affect the observed rates. I would compare the grouped results with continuous-age and continuous-wait analyses to verify that the broad patterns are not artifacts of binning.

### What Worked Well

1. **Starting with data-quality checks.** Dimensions, missing values, duplicate rows, descriptive statistics, and the original target counts established a baseline before transformations were applied.

2. **Making the target encoding explicit.** The derived `no_show` variable prevents a common semantic error and makes rates easy to interpret as the mean of a binary indicator.

3. **Pairing rates with group sizes.** Reporting totals and no-show counts alongside percentages gives readers the denominator context needed to assess the comparisons.

4. **Using a structured set of research questions.** The demographics/health, waiting-time, and SMS sections keep the EDA focused and connect the visualizations to concrete questions.

5. **Comparing SMS status within waiting-time groups.** This is more informative than relying on the raw SMS/no-SMS difference alone, while still respecting the limits of observational data.

6. **Using executed notebook outputs to correct the report.** The notebook provides a consistent reference for numeric claims, helping expose outdated figures and contradictory narrative statements.

### The AI Partnership

> "The value of AI in this project was not simply to make an HTML report look polished. It was to help reconcile a narrative artifact with the executed analysis and to organize the evidence into a coherent explanation. The notebook had to remain the authority for the figures; the report needed to reflect those figures without quietly inventing a better story. The human role was to insist on that evidence hierarchy, notice the significance of the 38,568-row exclusion, and preserve the distinction between descriptive association and causal explanation. The AI accelerated comparison and drafting, but it did not remove the need to verify the charts or challenge the cleaning assumptions."

The most useful lesson is that **a report can be numerically updated without being fully validated**. Correct text is only one part of correctness: the data transformation, denominators, visual geometry, and interpretation all need to agree.

### Final Thoughts

This project demonstrates the core discipline of exploratory data analysis: inspect the source, make transformations explicit, ask focused questions, compare groups using both counts and rates, and keep conclusions proportional to the evidence. In the retained dataset, longer appointment waits are associated with higher no-show rates, the youngest age bands have higher observed rates than older groups, and SMS recipients have lower observed rates overall and within the longer waiting-time groups.

The most important engineering lesson is also the most important analytical caveat: the cleaning rule removes **38,568 of 110,527 original rows** because `wait_days < 0` or `age < 0`. Until that exclusion is understood and its impact tested, the observed rates should be presented as findings about the retained data—not automatically as representative estimates for all appointments. The HTML report has been updated to reflect the executed figures, but its SVG charts still require direct validation against the notebook before final publication.
